Bernoulli’s Theorem

For a steady, smooth flow of air along a streamline, an increase in the speed of the air comes with a decrease in its pressure.

P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

  • P static pressure: the pressure the air exerts on a surface
  • ½ρv² dynamic pressure from the air’s motion (ρ = air density, v = speed)
  • ρgh height term, negligible across the thickness of a wing

Because the total stays constant, if v rises then P must fall. Air moving faster over the top of a wing therefore has lower pressure than the slower air underneath. The theorem assumes steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, which is a good approximation for an airliner’s wing at everyday speeds.

Stall. The airflow has separated from the top surface, so lift collapses and drag rises. Lowering the nose reduces the angle and recovers the flow.

Angle of attack

The angle between the wing’s chord line (an imaginary line from its leading edge to its trailing edge) and the direction of the oncoming air. Increasing it makes the wing deflect more air downward and speeds up the flow over the top, so the pressure difference and the lift grow. Drag grows as well.

What the green arrow shows

Lift is the net upward force on the wing. It is the sum of the pressure differences over the whole wing surface: lower pressure above and higher pressure below. It acts roughly perpendicular to the oncoming air.

Stall

Beyond the critical angle (about 15° for many wings) the air can no longer follow the top surface and breaks away into turbulence. The low-pressure region collapses, lift drops sharply and drag climbs. Drag the slider past 15° to see it happen.

Equilibrium

The Four Forces

In steady, level flight the forces balance: lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag.

Lift

Acts on the wings. Produced by the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces. Opposes weight.

Weight

Acts at the centre of gravity. Gravity pulling the whole aircraft toward the Earth.

Thrust

Acts at the engines. Pushes the aircraft forward by accelerating air backward. Opposes drag.

Drag

Acts against the motion. Air resistance over the fuselage and wings.

Another view of lift

Newton’s Third Law

The tilted wing pushes air downward. The air pushes back on the wing with an equal and opposite force, upward. Bernoulli and Newton are two descriptions of the same lift: the pressure difference is how the wing transfers that push to the aircraft.

Common misconceptions

Myth Buster

Myth

Air over the top must travel farther, so it speeds up to meet the air from below at the trailing edge.

Truth

There is no rule that the two airflows must meet. In experiments the air over the top arrives first. The wing’s shape and angle speed up the flow, not an “equal transit time”.

Myth

Only a curved wing can create lift, so a flat wing cannot fly.

Truth

A flat paper plane flies thanks to its angle of attack. Curvature (camber) makes a wing more efficient, and aerobatic planes with symmetric wings can even fly upside down.

Myth

The engines push the plane up into the sky.

Truth

Engines provide thrust, which pushes the plane forward. The wings turn that forward speed into lift. Gliders have no engine at all and still stay up.

Myth

Bernoulli and Newton give competing explanations of lift, and only one is right.

Truth

Both are correct. They describe the same physics: the wing turns air downward (Newton), which creates the pressure difference that Bernoulli relates to speed.
